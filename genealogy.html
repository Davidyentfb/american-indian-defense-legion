One portal, kept under guard.

The Legion operates a genealogy portal for this work: your family's record in one place, built only from historical public records, and never published by us. It starts from what you know and grows only by what the archives can prove.

Family Trees

Private-by-default trees with parent and spouse relationships, built person by person from documented records — never from guesswork or other families' trees.

Person Profiles

An editable profile for every ancestor: names, dates, places, relationships, and the notes and citations that stand behind each fact.

People Index

Every person in your research, indexed and searchable, so a name found in one record can be followed into every other record that carries it.

Archival Search

On-demand searching of the federal archives — archives.gov, loc.gov, and bia.gov — shaped by the four collections below, on the question you are actually asking.

Source Leads

Promising records are saved as leads and kept, so research resumes where it stopped instead of starting over — and dead ends stay marked as dead ends.

Roll-Research Guides

Standing guides to the National Archives' Native American records: the Final Rolls, the Guion Miller index, the Kern-Clifton and Wallace Rolls, the Eastern Cherokee census rolls of 1835–1884, and the regional BIA finding aids.

The record sets we work.

Four collections carry most of what survives on paper. The portal searches them as one body of evidence, because a single life usually appears in several of them at once.

Dawes Rolls & Enrollment Records

The Final Rolls of the Five Civilized Tribes and the enrollment records around them: applications, census cards, and jackets — the first place most families are told to look, and the place that demands the most care in reading.

Deliverable: roll citations · enrollment-card references · application-jacket leads

Indian Census Rolls

The annual Indian census rolls taken by agency, year after year — households, ages, relationships, and allotments recorded in sequence, so a family can be followed across decades rather than found once.

Deliverable: household sequences · year-by-year residence trail

BIA Agency Records

The Bureau of Indian Affairs' agency files: correspondence, heirship and probate material, allotment records, and the regional finding aids that show what an agency kept and where it rests now.

Deliverable: agency-file leads · finding-aid references · probate trails

Indian School Records

Student files from the boarding-school era and the day schools: enrollment cards, attendance, and correspondence home. Hard records to read, and often the only ones that name a child directly.

Deliverable: student-file citations · school and year references

How the research runs.

Four steps, in order, every time. The discipline is the service: nothing enters a tree without a record beneath it.

The method

From a family's first names to a documented line.

  1. Begin the tree.We enter what your family already knows — names, dates, places — marked as family knowledge, not yet as fact.
  2. Search the archives.The four collections are searched on demand, person by person, against the official federal sources.
  3. Cite every finding.A fact joins the tree only with its record attached: collection, roll, page, and archive. Uncited claims stay out.
  4. Keep the leads.Records that almost fit are saved as leads with the reason they were kept, so the next session — or the next generation — starts ahead.

The rules of the house

These do not bend, for any client, at any price.

Historical public records onlyThe portal is built on the archival record. Nothing is scraped from the living, and nothing is invented to fill a gap.
Living persons private by defaultThe living appear in a tree only as relationships require, and their details are never the product.
Sources cited, alwaysIf a claim cannot be pointed to a record, it is labeled unverified or left out. A short tree that is true outranks a long one that is not.
Citizenship is not ours to decideThe Legion documents lineage. It does not determine tribal enrollment or citizenship — those decisions belong to tribes alone, and no finding here should be represented as one.

Begin with one name.
